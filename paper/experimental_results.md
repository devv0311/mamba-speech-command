## Experiment 3 — Noise robustness

**Measured, real run, 2026-08-24, on `mamba_run1` / `gru_run1` checkpoints
(both trained on clean audio only, no noise augmentation), evaluated on
the full 3,276-sample test set at each condition. Additive white Gaussian
noise, reproducible fixed seed (42), SNR computed relative to each
peak-normalized waveform's own signal power (see
`src/audio/noise.py:add_white_noise_at_snr`).**

| Condition | SNR (dB) | Mamba accuracy | Mamba macro F1 | GRU accuracy | GRU macro F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clean | — | 0.9451 | 0.9450 | 0.9399 | 0.9395 |
| Mild | 20 | 0.6575 | 0.6480 | 0.8196 | 0.8197 |
| Moderate | 10 | 0.1596 | 0.0847 | 0.2863 | 0.2000 |
| Strong | 0 | 0.1404 | 0.0487 | 0.1325 | 0.0413 |

(Chance-level accuracy for 8 balanced classes is 0.125; both models
approach chance at 0 dB SNR.)

**Observation:** the GRU baseline is more noise-robust than Mamba at mild
and moderate noise despite Mamba's small clean-audio accuracy advantage —
GRU retains substantially more accuracy at 20 dB (81.96% vs 65.75%) and
10 dB (28.63% vs 15.96%) SNR. At 0 dB both models collapse to
near-chance performance. Neither model was trained with noise
augmentation, so this reflects each architecture's inherent robustness to
an out-of-distribution condition, not a tuned robustness comparison. This
result runs counter to a naive "Mamba is simply better" reading of
Experiments 1-2 and is reported as observed — no causal explanation for
the robustness gap is claimed without further investigation (e.g.
noise-augmented training, which is out of scope for this initial
comparison).

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| End-to-end (total) latency | mean 56.1 ms, median 49.4 ms, min 46.8 ms, max 116.6 ms (n=10) |
| Model inference latency | mean 55.9 ms, median 49.5 ms (n=10) |

**Observation — live prediction confidence:** the first prediction (116.6
ms) is noticeably higher than the rest and is likely one-time MPS graph/
kernel warm-up on the first live forward pass, not representative
steady-state latency; predictions 2-10 cluster tightly at 46-54 ms.
Separately, several live confidences are low (many in the 22-40% range,
one exactly 50.0%) compared to the 94.5% clean test-set accuracy in
Experiment 1, and at least one predicted label ("left", "down" appearing
twice) does not obviously match a straightforward read-through of the 8
distinct commands. This checkpoint was trained only on the pre-segmented,
1-second Google Speech Commands clips; live microphone audio differs in
leading/trailing silence position, mic gain, and room acoustics, none of
which the model saw during training or noise-robustness testing. This is
recorded as an honest, unresolved observation — live transcript-level
accuracy (predicted command vs. what was actually spoken, in order) is
NOT tracked here because the exact spoken sequence was not logged
alongside the run; only latency, confidence, and the printed predicted
labels are real measured values. A follow-up run with an explicit spoken
sequence log would be needed to report live accuracy as a number.

### Post-fix re-verification (measured, real run, 2026-08-24)

Following the capture-alignment fix documented in
`paper/methodology_notes.md` §8 (VAD-triggered snapshot was capturing the
word at the trailing edge of the 1-second window rather than centered, as
training clips are; fixed by waiting `realtime.post_trigger_wait_sec`
after trigger before snapshotting), the demo was re-run with the same
speaker reading through the vocabulary (26 predictions captured, same
live-mic/MPS/batch=1 setup, on the STEP 12 clean-rebuilt environment):

Raw after-fix predictions (predicted / confidence / total latency ms):
up 38.7%/153.5, up 49.4%/75.6, up 29.7%/66.4, stop 29.7%/72.0,
down 85.3%/67.6, go 96.8%/75.3, left 83.3%/67.7, up 46.8%/75.0,

**Honest framing:** confidence rose substantially after the fix, and many
predictions now sit at 94-99.7%, consistent with the capture-alignment
hypothesis. This is NOT a verified accuracy figure — the exact spoken
sequence was still not logged alongside the run, so predicted-vs-actual
correctness per utterance is not computed here, only the confidence/
latency pattern. Several low-confidence predictions remain (two `up` at
33-39%, three `stop` at 31-45%), left as an open, unresolved observation
rather than smoothed over — the fix is a heuristic alignment improvement
(see `paper/methodology_notes.md` §8), not an exact reproduction of the
dataset's trimming protocol, so some residual mismatch is expected. Total
latency increased (mean 56.4ms -> 74.3ms) as the direct, expected cost of
the added `post_trigger_wait_sec` delay; all 26/26 predictions remained
within the 200ms real-time criterion.

## Pre-training timing probe (measured, real Mac, 2026-08-24)

From `scripts/benchmark.py --probe-only`, batch_size=64 (the configured
training batch size), single forward+backward pass:

| Backbone | Device | fwd+bwd (ms) | Estimated epoch time (24,713 train samples) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mamba | MPS | 1463.7 | 565.0s (9.4 min) |
| Mamba | CPU | 2560.0 | 988.2s (16.5 min) |
| GRU | MPS | 70.4 | 27.2s (0.5 min) |
| GRU | CPU | 30.6 | 11.8s (0.2 min) |

The MPS Mamba epoch-time estimate (9.4 min) closely matched the real
measured per-epoch time in the actual training run above (575-602 sec =
9.6-10.0 min), confirming the probe methodology is reliable. Mamba is
substantially slower than GRU per batch — an architecturally-expected
consequence of the sequential-scan implementation not using a fused
hardware-aware kernel (see `paper/methodology_notes.md` §7) — this is
reported as a real trade-off, not hidden or minimized.

At batch=8 (smaller than the configured training batch size), MPS was
NOT faster than CPU for Mamba (237ms vs 198ms) — likely GPU dispatch
overhead dominating at small batch sizes; at batch=64 MPS was clearly
faster for both backbones. This batch-size-dependent crossover is noted
as an observed characteristic of this implementation on this hardware.

## Full benchmark sweep (measured, real Mac, 2026-08-24)

**`scripts/benchmark.py` (full sweep, not probe-only). Both backbones x
both devices (MPS, CPU) x batch sizes {1, 8, 16, 64}. `fwd+bwd` = one
forward+backward pass (training-representative); `inference` columns are
forward-only (eval-representative), with per-sample time in parentheses.
Raw data: `experiments/results/benchmark_full.json`.**

| Backbone | Device | Batch | fwd+bwd (ms) | Inference total (ms) | Inference (ms/sample) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mamba | MPS | 1 | 108.2 | 20.65 | 20.65 |
| Mamba | MPS | 8 | 239.7 | 27.49 | 3.44 |
| Mamba | MPS | 16 | 421.7 | 33.42 | 2.09 |
| Mamba | MPS | 64 | 1486.3 | 66.00 | 1.03 |
| Mamba | CPU | 1 | 77.2 | 13.69 | 13.69 |
| Mamba | CPU | 8 | 192.1 | 27.87 | 3.48 |
| Mamba | CPU | 16 | 686.5 | 209.81 | 13.11 |
| Mamba | CPU | 64 | 2570.6 | 445.62 | 6.96 |
| GRU | MPS | 1 | 87.6 | 16.22 | 16.22 |
| GRU | MPS | 8 | 83.8 | 19.86 | 2.48 |
| GRU | MPS | 16 | 68.3 | 20.73 | 1.30 |
| GRU | MPS | 64 | 70.5 | 21.21 | 0.33 |
| GRU | CPU | 1 | 10.0 | 1.33 | 1.33 |
| GRU | CPU | 8 | 12.6 | 3.15 | 0.39 |
| GRU | CPU | 16 | 14.4 | 4.21 | 0.26 |
| GRU | CPU | 64 | 28.2 | 11.40 | 0.18 |

**Observations:**

- GRU on CPU is faster than GRU on MPS at every tested batch size (e.g.
  batch=64: 11.40ms CPU vs 21.21ms MPS inference) — for a model this
  small, MPS dispatch/kernel-launch overhead outweighs any parallelism
  benefit. This is reported as measured, not smoothed over: MPS is not
  unconditionally the right device choice for the GRU baseline on this
  hardware.
- Mamba shows the opposite crossover: CPU is faster at batch=1 (13.69ms
  vs 20.65ms) and roughly comparable at batch=8, but MPS pulls
  meaningfully ahead at batch=16 and batch=64 (33.42ms / 66.00ms MPS vs
  209.81ms / 445.62ms CPU) — consistent with the batch-size-dependent
  crossover noted in the earlier probe.
- Mamba/CPU/batch=16's inference figure (209.81ms) is anomalously high
  relative to its own batch=8 (27.87ms) and batch=64 (445.62ms, i.e.
  ~27.8ms/sample) neighbors — 13.11ms/sample vs a roughly linear ~3-7ms/
  sample trend elsewhere in the CPU row. This looks like a measurement
  artifact (e.g. transient background load on the Mac during that one
  run) rather than a genuine architectural cliff at that specific batch
  size, but it has not been independently re-verified with a repeat run,
  so it is reported as-measured rather than corrected or discarded.
- Per-sample inference cost drops sharply with batch size for both
  backbones on MPS (Mamba: 20.65ms/sample at batch=1 -> 1.03ms/sample at
  batch=64), reflecting real batching efficiency; the real-time demo
  (Experiment 4) runs at batch=1, so its ~46-56ms measured latency is the
  operationally relevant number for the live single-utterance use case,
  not the batched throughput figures here.
